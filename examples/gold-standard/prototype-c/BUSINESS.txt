FICTIONAL. The company, its people, its mill and its stockists are invented. The building
science, the pigments and the paint chemistry are real, and are described accurately, because a
customer who has been sold the wrong paint for a solid wall has usually been sold it by
somebody who was vague about exactly this.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
THE PRODUCT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Marlpit makes mineral paint: lime-based, breathable, for buildings whose walls were never
designed to be sealed.

A solid brick or stone wall with no cavity manages moisture by letting it move — in when it
rains, out when it dries. It has done this for two hundred years. Modern paint is a plastic
film: acrylic or vinyl, formulated to be washable and to stay put, and formulated therefore not
to let water vapour through. Put it on a solid wall and the wall stops drying outward. The
moisture goes somewhere else — into the plaster, the timber, the mortar.

That is not a marketing claim. It is the reason a Victorian terrace that was fine for a century
starts showing damp two years after somebody repainted it.

Marlpit sells four mineral paints — limewash, silicate, clay distemper and a lime-casein — in
thirty-six colours, all of them made with pigments that survive an alkaline binder. That last
constraint is the whole reason the palette looks the way it does, and the site says so.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. WHAT PROBLEM IT SOLVES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Two problems, and they are usually the same customer six months apart.

The technical one: getting a breathable coating onto a wall that needs one, at a specification
that will actually hold, on a substrate that has probably been painted with something else
already.

The one people actually arrive with: choosing a colour. Lime paint is not flat colour. It is
pigment suspended in lime, applied thin, and it moves under light in a way a plastic paint
does not — the same wall is a different colour at nine in the morning and at four in the
afternoon, and different again under a lamp. People choose from a printed card in a shop, paint
a room, and hate it.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. WHO USES IT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

  - owners of solid-wall houses — pre-1919 terraces, cottages, farm buildings — who have been
    told the wall needs to breathe and do not yet know what that means for the tin;
  - conservation officers and heritage architects specifying for listed work;
  - builders and decorators who have been asked for lime and have not applied it before, and
    who need the substrate and coverage answers before they quote;
  - a small number of interior designers, who are the least of the three commercially and the
    loudest.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3. WHAT IT REPLACES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

  - trade emulsion on a wall that should not have it;
  - a printed colour card, which cannot show a colour moving under light and is the direct
    cause of most returned orders in this trade;
  - a phone call to a technical line to ask whether it can go over what is already there;
  - the assumption, held by nearly everybody, that "breathable" is a marketing word.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4. THE BEHAVIOUR THAT DIFFERENTIATES IT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

It shows the colour moving, and it tells you the shown colour is not good enough.

Every colour on this site is rendered under four named light conditions, computed from a
stated chromatic transform rather than picked by eye. It is an approximation and the site says
so on the same screen — which is not a disclaimer but the argument: if a computed rendering on
a calibrated screen is still not the answer, a printed card in a shop under fluorescent light
certainly is not, and the only honest way to choose is a sample pot on your own wall.

The second thing: it will tell you a colour is wrong for your substrate, and which of the four
paints is not available in it, before you have chosen anything.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5. WHAT IS IMPRESSIVE SHOWN RATHER THAN DESCRIBED
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The same colour, four times, visibly different.

Everything a paint company says about light is unfalsifiable prose until you put the four
renderings next to each other at a size where the difference is undeniable. Then the sentence
"north light is cooler and takes the warmth out of an earth pigment" stops being copy and
becomes a thing on the screen you are looking at.

The second is the pigment itself. Thirty-six colours from six pigment families, and the
families are visible: the palette has no bright greens and no clean violets, because the
pigments that survive lime do not produce them. A constraint you can see is worth more than a
paragraph about authenticity.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
6. OBJECTIONS THE SITE MUST ANSWER
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

"Is 'breathable' actually a real thing or is it a marketing word."
  Real, measurable, and stated as a number: vapour resistance, in MNs/g, for each paint,
  against the figure a solid wall needs.

"Can it go over what is already on my wall."
  Usually not, and the site says so before it takes an order. There is a substrate matrix, and
  the honest answer for a wall in modern emulsion is that it must come off first.

"It will look chalky and it will rub off on my clothes."
  Limewash does, to a degree, for the first weeks and in some finishes permanently. Which of
  the four does and which does not is stated per paint rather than glossed.

"It costs more."
  It does. Per litre, and less per square metre than it looks, because coverage differs. The
  calculator uses the real coverage figures rather than the flattering ones.

"Colour on a screen is meaningless."
  Correct. That is why the whole conversion is a sample pot rather than a basket.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
7. EVIDENCE AVAILABLE HONESTLY FROM THE MODEL
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

  - thirty-six colours with real pigment provenance, light reflectance values and lime-fastness;
  - four paints with real vapour resistance figures, coverage rates and substrate limits;
  - a computed rendering of every colour under four illuminants, with the transform stated;
  - a substrate compatibility matrix that refuses combinations rather than upselling them;
  - coverage and cost per square metre, computed from the real figures.

No invented awards. No press quotes. No interiors influencer.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
8. THE CONVERSION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Sample pots. Four for £22, delivered, and the site says plainly that this is the only way to
choose and that the screen you are reading is not.

The secondary conversion, for the trade, is a specification: send the building, get a written
substrate and product recommendation from somebody who has stood in front of one.

THE COMPANY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Marlpit. A mill on the Nadder, in Wiltshire, in chalk country — which is why it is there, since
the lime comes from four miles away. Founded 2009 by a building conservator who could not buy
a limewash she was willing to specify. Nineteen people. They make everything they sell.
